Your jobs today

Warm-up · How we sit to listen

Still bodies, looking at the speaker or the player. One listen with eyes. Name what we will listen for: drum, voice, fiddle, or dance feet.

Teach

Respect first

Some songs are sacred or family-owned. In this class we listen. We do not pretend, costume, or make up “Indigenous-sounding” chants. If a local Nation or Métis artist has shared a song for schools, we credit them by name.

One focus listen

Play 45–90 seconds. Ask: drum or fiddle? Fast or slow? For dancing or for sitting? Accept kid words.

Métis fiddle and jig is one good door

If you use a jig, students may pat a beat or try a simple step only if you are teaching a school-appropriate dance, not a mockery. Movement is optional. Listening is the job.

Many cultures

If time, a second short piece from another place the class has a real link to (a classmate’s family, a book you read). Same respectful frame.

Optional story

The listening chair

Sam wanted to bang along the first time the drum came through the speaker. Ms. Cole lifted the quiet hand. “This drum already has players,” she said. “Today we borrow our ears, not their song.” Sam sat on his hands and heard the pattern he had missed. After, he said the drum was like walking. That was enough.

Story suggestion

We Belong to the Drum · Sandra Lamouche (illus. Azby Whitecalf)

Cree-authored story about the drum, belonging, and culturally safe listening—not a sacred-song how-to.

Cultural care: listen and appreciate only; do not teach or copy sacred songs.

Buy on Amazon · Watch the author read the opening (sample) (Baker & Taylor on YouTube)

How to use this book

When: Hook for Teach, sitting in listening posture (about 7 min). Read it as a story to listen to. It isn’t a song to copy.

Before reading

  • “Nikosis heard the drum before he was born, in his mother’s heartbeat. Put a hand on your heart. Can you feel yours?”
  • “Today our job is to listen and to care.”

During reading

  • Read it straight through as a listening story. No drumming along and no pretend powwow singing.
  • Stop once when Nikosis feels scared at daycare. “How do you think he feels? What might help?”
  • When the teacher plays powwow music and he feels he belongs, ask: “How did the music help him?”
  • Show the Plains Cree words in the book, and credit the author, Sandra Lamouche, and the illustrator, Azby Whitecalf.

After reading

Play a short recording shared by a named artist, then Draw what you heard (dots for drum, lines for voice). Share one noticing with a partner.

Quick check: “What does a respectful listener look and sound like?”

Adapt: Show the pictures slowly and let children point to what they notice. Some children may connect to their own family music. Welcome that without asking them to perform.

Extend: Invite a local Indigenous musician or Elder through your school’s protocols, and follow their lead.

Activity · Draw what you heard

A listening map: dots for drum, line for voice or fiddle. Share one noticing with a partner. No performing of the recording.

Close

Credit the artists again. “Thank you for the listen.” Stamp if the room stayed in listening mode.

Materials

  • One short recording you have rights to play (school library, licensed service, or a visiting artist)
  • A map of Turtle Island / Canada
  • Listening map paper

Teacher notes

  • Do not use random uncredited clips. Name the performer and Nation or community if it is known and appropriate.
  • If you are not sure a song is okay to teach, do not teach it. Listening with credit is the safe, good Grade 1 move.
  • Invite a local artist through proper school channels if you can — even a 15-minute visit beats a shaky recording.

Watch-outs

  • Making feather headbands or fake drums. Never.
  • Calling all First Nations music “powwow.” Be specific or stay general with respect.

Challenge

Write a thank-you sentence to the musicians we heard.

Optional faith note

Optional: listening without grabbing is a way to honour our neighbours.
